I've now sat through enough re-orgs, team splits, and monolith-to-microservices restructurings that I can tell you the exact week people stop asking "what does this mean for the product" and start asking "what does this mean for me." It's usually week two.

Managing a QA team through a transition like that is a strange job. Your job title says quality, but for a few months your actual job is trust maintenance. Process changes fast. Ownership boundaries move. Yesterday's "who owns this bug" answer is wrong today. And people can absorb maybe a tenth of that speed before they start protecting themselves instead of the product.

The thing I got wrong early in my career: I used to over-explain the plan. Big deck, clear boundaries, here's the new org chart. People nodded and then went back to their desks anxious anyway, because a re-org isn't actually a communications problem, it's a certainty problem, and I was handing them a plan I couldn't fully guarantee.

What's worked better since: saying the true, smaller thing out loud. "I don't know exactly how the ownership split lands yet, but you'll hear it from me the day I know, not the day it's official." Saying it about layoffs, when that's what it is, instead of dressing it in "synergy" language. People forgive uncertainty. They don't forgive finding out you knew and sat on it.

The technical side of a monolith-to-microservices split is genuinely interesting work. But the reason some of those migrations stall isn't the architecture. It's that the humans on the two new teams stopped trusting each other's "done" somewhere around month three, and nobody in the room was doing the unglamorous work of keeping that trust alive while the ground moved.
