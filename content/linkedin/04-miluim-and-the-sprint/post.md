Try explaining sprint velocity to a scrum tool when a third of your team just got a phone call and disappeared for three weeks. There's no story point for that, and after enough years in Israeli tech you stop expecting one.

Reserve duty, miluim, is one of those things that never shows up in a job description but shapes half the actual planning conversations I have. Someone on my team gets called up mid-sprint, sometimes mid-incident, and the honest answer is: the sprint plan was always a guess, and now it's a guess with a hole in it. You don't get to be precious about the roadmap. You get to be good at re-planning on short notice, and calm about it, because the person walking out the door has enough on their mind without also managing your disappointment.

It's connected to something bigger about working here that's hard to describe to people outside it: a kind of intensity and informality at the same time. Standups where someone's blunt to your face about a bad decision, five minutes after asking about your kids by name. Building software normally while the country is, very much, not going through normal times. You learn to hold both. The work still matters, the deadline is still real, and also a person just left to do reserve duty and the deadline is going to move, and that's fine, actually, that's just what this looks like here.

I don't think there's a clean lesson in this one. Just that resilience in a team isn't really about processes surviving disruption. It's about people trusting that when the disruption is a human one, the team responds like humans, not like a burndown chart.
