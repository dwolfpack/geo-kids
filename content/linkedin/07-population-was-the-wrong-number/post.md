I built the difficulty levels for my daughter's geography game around population. It felt like the obviously correct, defensible, "objective" choice. It was completely wrong, and it took watching her actually play the game to see it.

The original split was five levels of countries, roughly 30/30/30/5/5, ordered by population size. Big populous countries first, small ones last, easy to justify in a design doc, easy to compute, hard to argue with on paper. Then I watched a seven-year-old breeze through "hard" countries and get stuck on ones I'd ranked easy. Bangladesh has a bigger population than plenty of countries she named instantly. She'd never heard of it. Meanwhile small countries she absolutely knew, because a cousin visited once, or she'd seen the flag somewhere, or it came up in a book, were sitting in the wrong tier entirely.

Population was measuring something real. It just wasn't measuring the thing that determines whether a kid can win. That's a recognizability problem, not a population problem, and no dataset I had access to captured "has a seven-year-old plausibly heard of this place." I had to go tier by tier and rebuild it by judgment, ending up with a much more even 39/39/39/39/38 split across the five levels, ordered by what a kid actually recognizes rather than what a census counts.

This keeps happening to me at work in less cute forms. The metric that's easiest to pull and hardest to argue with in a meeting is rarely the metric that predicts the outcome you actually care about. Lines of code, ticket count, population size, pick your poison. Objective and relevant are not the same word, no matter how much easier it is to build a chart when they are.
