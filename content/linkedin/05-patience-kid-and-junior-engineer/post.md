My six-year-old wanted to know why Bolivia's capital wasn't showing up right in the geography game I'd been building her. I'd explained the same country-picker logic to her three times already that week. On the fourth, instead of explaining it again, I just watched her click around for a minute. She found the actual problem herself in about twenty seconds. I would've kept talking for five more minutes if she'd let me.

I've managed junior engineers for a long time, and I only recently noticed how much of that same instinct applies at work. When someone's stuck, my default used to be to explain harder. Louder version of the same explanation, more detail, more context, as if the first explanation just needed volume. What actually helps, most of the time, is shutting up and letting them drive for a bit, even slowly, even wrong, because the thing they find on their own sticks in a way the thing I told them never does.

Building this game with my kids has been a strange little management workshop. Explaining what a "capital" even means to a kid, in a way that's true but not overwhelming, is not so different from explaining to a junior why a test failed in a way that teaches instead of just fixes it for them. Both require you to sit in the uncomfortable silence while someone else works something out, instead of filling the silence because it's easier for you.

I still get it wrong plenty. I still jump in too fast, at home and at work. But the geography game has quietly become my reminder that the best teaching moment is usually the one where I said less than I wanted to.
