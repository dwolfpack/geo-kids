# Keeping Trust When the Process Changes Faster Than People Can Absorb It

### Notes from managing a team through a re-org, a monolith split, and the quiet damage of moving too fast

There's a specific kind of silence in a team meeting when people have stopped asking questions not because they understand, but because they've given up on getting an answer that helps. I've heard that silence more than once in my career, and I've caused it at least once myself, which is a harder thing to admit than to write.

I've managed QA teams through a few different flavors of upheaval — a re-org that redrew reporting lines overnight, a monolith-to-microservices migration that redrew ownership boundaries along with them, and the more painful kind of transition, where headcount shrinks and the people who remain have to absorb both the workload and the grief of the ones who didn't. Each of these looked different on the org chart. All of them ran into the exact same human problem: process can change in a single announcement. Trust changes at the speed of lived experience, and those two speeds are never the same.

## The mistake: mistaking the announcement for the change

When my team's testing responsibilities got restructured around the new service boundaries, I did what I think most managers do the first time: I explained the new structure clearly, once, in a well-organized meeting, with a diagram. I thought clarity was the job. I was wrong about what the job actually was.

Two weeks later I discovered that half the team was still filing bugs the old way, against the old ownership map, because the diagram had told them what changed but nothing had shown them, in practice, that the old way would actually stop working. People don't update their mental model because you told them to. They update it when the new model survives contact with a real situation and the old one visibly doesn't. My mistake wasn't the plan. It was assuming one clear explanation could do the work of a dozen lived repetitions.

What actually worked, once I adjusted, was smaller and slower than I wanted: sitting with individual testers on their first few bugs under the new structure, walking the actual routing with them in real time, letting them get it slightly wrong once in a low-stakes way rather than getting it wrong once in front of an escalation. Repetition beats explanation, every time, when what you're asking people to change is a habit, not just a fact they know.

## Layoffs teach you that competence isn't the same as reassurance

The hardest transition I've managed wasn't a technical restructuring. It was watching a team shrink and then having to lead the people who stayed, most of whom were now doing the work of colleagues they'd worked beside for years and genuinely missed. My instinct, as someone whose whole career has been about finding what's actually broken and fixing it, was to focus on the practical: redistribute the workload fairly, be transparent about what got cut and why, make sure nobody was quietly drowning.

That instinct wasn't wrong, but it was incomplete. People who'd just watched colleagues leave weren't primarily asking "is the workload distributed fairly." They were asking, underneath everything else, "am I next, and would anyone tell me honestly if I were." No amount of competent redistribution answers that question. Only consistent, unglamorous honesty over time does — showing up, saying what you actually know and admitting what you don't, and not disappearing into your own stress when the team most needed to see that someone was still steady.

I told my team, more than once, some version of "I don't have an answer to that, and I'm not going to pretend I do to make this conversation easier." That sentence cost me some short-term comfort. It bought long-term trust, because the alternative — false reassurance that turns out to be wrong — spends trust you don't get back cheaply.

## Chutzpah is a feature, not a management problem

One thing I've come to appreciate managing teams in Israel specifically: people push back. Loudly, sometimes bluntly, often in the middle of a meeting you'd expected to be a status update. Early in my management career I read that pushback as a threat to the plan. It isn't. It's information you'd otherwise have to go dig for yourself. A team that argues with a re-org plan in the room is a team that's still invested enough to fight for a better version of it. The team you should actually worry about is the quiet one, the one that's stopped bothering to push back because they've concluded it won't change anything.

The best re-org I ran wasn't the one with the smoothest kickoff meeting. It was the one where three different engineers told me, in three different blunt ways, that a piece of my plan didn't make sense — and they were right, and the plan changed because of it, and I said so out loud afterward. Crediting the pushback publicly did more for trust in that transition than any all-hands slide could have.

## What actually earns trust in a transition

If I had to compress what I've learned into one thing, it's this: people forgive a hard change. They don't forgive being managed as if the change were easy. Say plainly what's uncertain. Repeat the new reality until it's a habit, not a memo. Let people push back, especially when it's uncomfortable, and let them see that pushback occasionally wins. None of that makes the transition painless. It just makes it survivable with the team's trust in you mostly intact on the other side, which, in my experience, is the actual measure of whether you managed it well.
