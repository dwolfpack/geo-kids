I've now managed a team through a re-org, a layoff round, and a monolith-to-microservices split. Different causes, same wound: process changes faster than people can absorb it.

The mistake I made the first time was explaining the new org chart really well and thinking that counted as done. People didn't need the chart explained. They needed to know if they still had a seat in it.

You cannot out-communicate uncertainty with more slides. What actually calms a team down is smaller and slower: the same manager, saying true things, showing up on the same days, even when the true things are "I don't know yet, I'll tell you Thursday."

When we split the monolith team into service-owning squads, the technical work was the easy 30%. The hard 70% was that testers who used to know the whole system now had to trust people they barely worked with to catch the parts they could no longer see.

Trust doesn't transfer with a Slack announcement. It gets rebuilt one incident at a time, by watching whether the other team tells you about their bug before it becomes your outage.

During layoffs, the worst thing I did wasn't a decision, it was a silence. I went quiet for two days while things got finalized, trying to protect people from half-information. They filled that silence with worse guesses than the truth would've been.

I don't have a clean framework for this. What I have is: say less than you want to promise, more than you want to admit, and never let "I can't share details yet" turn into "I've gone dark."

People forgive a manager who doesn't have answers yet. They don't forgive one who disappears while they're waiting for them.
