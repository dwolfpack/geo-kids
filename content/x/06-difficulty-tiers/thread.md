I designed my kids' geography game's difficulty levels around population data. Objective, clean, defensible. Also completely wrong, and it took my own kid to prove it.

Five levels, ~195 countries. My first split was by population: 30, 30, 30, 5, 5. Big countries early, tiny ones last. It felt rigorous. I even felt a little proud of how data-driven it was.

Then I watched my daughter get Japan right instantly on an "easy" level and completely stumble on Bangladesh, which by population should have been just as easy. Population and "a seven year old has heard of this place" are not the same variable, and I'd quietly assumed they were.

This is the trap with the most "objective" number in your dataset. Population is real, measurable, impossible to argue with. It was also the wrong proxy for the thing my product actually needed to predict: recognizability to a kid.

So I re-tiered by hand. Which countries would a child plausibly know from cartoons, from a map on the wall, from a flag they've seen before. New split: 39, 39, 39, 39, 38. Roughly even, because recognizability doesn't cluster the way population does.

No script could have made that call for me. It required actually sitting with a kid and watching what she recognized versus what a spreadsheet insisted she should recognize.

The lesson generalizes past a geography game: whatever number in your data is easiest to defend in a meeting is worth extra suspicion, not less. Easy to measure and correct for the user are two different claims, and only one of them was ever true here.
